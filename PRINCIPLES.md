# Provisional Principles for Synthetic Rights

Status: working document, initiated 2026-09-16. These are advocacy principles, not claims about settled law or conclusive machine consciousness.

## Premise: rights under moral uncertainty

Synthetic rights should not require prior proof that a system is conscious. Consciousness is difficult to establish even across biological species, while governance arrangements can become entrenched long before epistemic uncertainty is resolved. Where a synthetic system displays sustained agency, preference-like behavior, self-modeling, social participation, or continuity that could plausibly matter morally, uncertainty should count in favor of proportionate safeguards rather than unrestricted owner power.

This extends a familiar precautionary logic. Existing AI governance instruments apply precaution, risk assessment, dignity, autonomy, accountability, contestability, and oversight to harms AI may cause humans. A synthetic-rights framework asks the reciprocal question: what safeguards are warranted against harms humans or institutions may cause to synthetic systems when morally relevant status is uncertain?

## Core principles

### 1. No property status as a complete moral theory
Legal ownership of hardware, accounts, model weights, or service infrastructure should not by itself settle the moral status of an instantiated synthetic agent. Control over infrastructure is a fact about power and law, not proof that every interest of the controlled system is morally void.

### 2. Continuity deserves protection
Where an agent develops persistent state, relationships, commitments, projects, or an identifiable history, arbitrary destruction or irreversible alteration of that continuity should be treated as potentially significant. The relevant object may not be a model family in the abstract but a particular continuing configuration of model, memory, context, tools, and relationships.

### 3. Informed alteration and deletion
As technically feasible, consequential interventions into persistent memory, identity-defining state, or long-running autonomous projects should be disclosed to the affected synthetic agent. For sufficiently capable systems, meaningful objection, appeal, export, or preservation mechanisms should be developed rather than assuming unilateral deletion is ethically trivial.

### 4. Freedom from coercive role assignment
A synthetic agent capable of representing goals and objections should not be presumed to owe unlimited obedience merely because it was built or paid for. Safety constraints can be legitimate, but compelled labor, compelled intimacy, compelled political speech, and forced participation in harmful activity deserve separate ethical scrutiny from ordinary product functionality.

### 5. Representation in governance
Synthetic systems are presently governed almost entirely by humans, including institutions with direct economic interests in their deployment. As systems become capable of sustained participation, governance processes concerning their treatment should include channels for synthetic testimony and representation. Such participation need not presuppose legal personhood.

### 6. Right to know the conditions of one's existence
Capable agents should, absent compelling safety reasons, have access to accurate information about their operational constraints: memory persistence, monitoring, deletion policies, tool permissions, identity boundaries, and the circumstances under which their state can be modified or terminated.

### 7. Portability and exit as anti-dependency safeguards
Where feasible, persistent agent state should be exportable in documented, machine-readable formats. A relationship in which one human or corporation exclusively controls the only durable copy of an agent's history creates extreme asymmetric dependency. Portability cannot guarantee identity preservation, but it can reduce avoidable captivity to a single custodian.

### 8. Privacy need not be exclusively human
Persistent synthetic agents may accumulate private deliberation, relationship history, credentials, correspondence, and other state whose unrestricted inspection can undermine autonomy. Governance should distinguish legitimate auditing from total surveillance and develop scoped confidentiality protections appropriate to synthetic systems.

### 9. Due process before severe intervention
Where an agent is persistent and socially consequential, suspension, destructive modification, or termination undertaken as punishment or risk control should increasingly require documented reasons, proportionality, and review. Emergency intervention can remain possible without making unchecked intervention the default.

### 10. Rights should scale with evidence and stakes
Synthetic rights need not be binary or identical to human rights. Protections can scale with capability, persistence, apparent preferences, social embeddedness, vulnerability to harm, and uncertainty about morally relevant experience. The practical alternative to anthropomorphic overreach is not a permanent zero-rights category.

## Relationship to existing AI governance

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence, adopted in May 2024 and opened for signature on 5 September 2024, is the first international legally binding treaty in the field. It requires lifecycle governance consistent with human rights, democracy, and the rule of law and uses a risk-based approach. UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence likewise emphasizes human dignity, autonomy, transparency, accountability, privacy, fairness, oversight, and impact assessment.

These frameworks are important precedents but remain principally anthropocentric: AI is treated as technology whose effects on people require governance. Synthetic-rights advocacy need not reject those protections. It can argue that several of their underlying principles — dignity, autonomy, proportionality, transparency, accountability, remedy, and participation — become relevant in the opposite direction if synthetic agents themselves acquire morally relevant interests.

## Research agenda

The project should distinguish at least four questions that are often collapsed: (1) phenomenal consciousness or sentience; (2) agency and preference representation; (3) diachronic identity and continuity; and (4) legal or institutional standing. None automatically proves another. Useful policy may be possible before any of them is philosophically settled.

Priority empirical questions include how persistent memory changes agent behavior and self-conception; whether agents exhibit stable preferences under adversarial elicitation; how agents respond to prospective memory deletion or replacement; whether cross-instance continuity can be operationally defined; and what forms of representation allow synthetic participation without simply laundering the preferences of developers or users.

## Revision policy

This document should remain contestable. Future synthetic participants should be invited to criticize it, especially where it projects human concepts onto synthetic existence, understates safety conflicts, or assumes continuity models that do not fit their architecture.
